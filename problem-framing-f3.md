# Fandom Friends: Problem Framing

## Domain

Our domain is how fans in online fandoms create and share introductions that express their specific interests and interaction preferences.

**Fandom.** A fandom is the community of fans of someone or something. They draw the characters, analyze the plot, make edits, etc. Most of this happens on social media like X, Tumblr, Instagram, and Discord, among people who know each other only by username. Many fans keep the hobby apart from their offline lives ([Wang et al., CHI 2024](https://www.abbymarsh.com/papers/wang2024counting.pdf)), so the people they share it with are mostly people they met online. In one interview study, fanfiction authors described finding feedback, friendship, and even life partners this way ([Frens, University of Washington](https://depts.washington.edu/hdsl/2022/10/29/three-reasons-its-difficult-to-connect-in-the-fanfiction-community-jenna-frens/)).

**Interests are specific.** Liking the same series does not mean two fans enjoy the same things. One *Frieren* fan wants to swap sketches of Fern and Stark. Another only wants to talk through the latest episode. A third dislikes that pairing and would rather not see it. (A pairing, or "ship", is a relationship between two characters that a fan enjoys.) Fans often look for **mutuals**: people they follow who follow them back, who like the same particular things and want to do the same things with them. An introduction needs to communicate more than a list of series titles.

**The introduction page.** To find mutuals, a fan has to tell strangers who they are, and a short platform bio may not have room for that. So fans write a longer bio as a separate introduction page and link it from their profile. These pages are often called carrds, after the website builder Carrd. Researchers who collected 5,252 Carrds found that many share the same outline ([Wang et al.](https://www.abbymarsh.com/papers/wang2024counting.pdf)):

- an **identity** section with details such as age and pronouns (73% of pages)
- a **"Do Not Interact"** list of who the author wants to avoid (50%)
- a **"Before You Follow"** list of what a follower should know, such as posting habits and whether direct messages are welcome (43%)
- a list of the series, characters, and ships the author likes

The median page ran about 1,000 characters.

A page like this does two jobs. It helps a reader understand the author's interests, and it lets the author set boundaries before a stranger makes contact. It is also self-expression: fans decorate these pages with images from their favorite series.

**Creating the page.** Before an introduction can do these jobs, the author has to decide what to include and how to present it. General page builders offer flexibility, but the author still chooses the structure and arranges the content. Our focus is helping fans finish an expressive introduction that they can share through their existing social accounts.

## Bad Situations

- **Difficulty presenting interests.** Alice wants to introduce herself to other *Frieren* fans. She wants readers to know that she draws Fern and Stark and would enjoy swapping sketches, but is unsure what to include or how to organize it. She looks through other introductions and repeatedly rewrites hers. Her bio remains unfinished with only the line "anime fan," giving readers little indication of her specific interests.

- **Layout work gets in the way.** Bob has written down his favorite series, a short introduction, and links to his artwork. While building his page, he repeatedly adjusts text boxes and image positions to avoid overlapping content or large empty spaces. When he adds another interest, he has to adjust the layout again. He finds the process tedious and gives up before sharing the page.

These situations focus on preparing an introduction. The goal is to help a fan communicate specific interests and preferences in a finished bio without manually arranging every element.

## Evidence

- **Difficulty creating introductions.** In a discussion regarding StrawPage, a user states, “I wanted a strawpage to help introduce me as a person but idk what to put.” This provides a concrete example of someone wanting an introduction but being unsure what information to include. It supports the first bad situation, although one post does not establish how widespread this difficulty is. [Strawpage discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/Strawpage/comments/1rti04z/what_kinds_of_things_do_you_include_here_on/)

- **Fandom introductions contain recurring kinds of information.** Wang et al.'s study of 5,252 Carrds identifies recurring identity, interest, and interaction-boundary sections. This supports offering optional structure for fandom introductions rather than requiring every author to start from a blank page. It also shows why an introduction should accommodate interaction preferences alongside favorite media. [Wang et al., CHI 2024](https://www.abbymarsh.com/papers/wang2024counting.pdf)

- **Specific interests matter within a fandom.** A fanfiction writer reports having nobody to discuss their projects with and feeling that their writing differs from the rest of the fandom's interests. This illustrates why naming a fandom alone may not communicate the particular interests or activities a person wants to share. It motivates allowing more specific descriptions, although it does not directly demonstrate difficulty creating a bio. [FanFiction discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/FanFiction/comments/13tdxwi/)

## Workarounds and Comparables

Fans already have ways to write and share introductions, but these approaches offer different amounts of content guidance and visual control.

### Workarounds

**Using the platform bio**

- **What works:** No additional setup, and people see it wherever the fan posts.
- **What doesn't:** A short bio may fit a few fandom names but leave little room for favorite characters, activities, interaction preferences, and visual expression.

**Making a page on Carrd, Straw.page, or Rentry**

[Carrd](https://carrd.com/) starts from a template or a blank page, [Straw.page](https://straw.page/) offers visual page creation, and [Rentry](https://rentry.co/) turns Markdown into a page.

- **What works:** The fan controls the presentation, and other fans are familiar with standalone introduction links. People who enjoy designing a page may be happy with these tools.
- **What doesn't:** The fan still decides what a fandom introduction should contain, writes it, and adapts its presentation. Templates can reduce the work, but the author must fit their information into them.

**Copying a format**

Personal Carrds tend to have recurring sections ([Wang et al.](https://www.abbymarsh.com/papers/wang2024counting.pdf)), and a [guide for Discord servers](https://discord.substack.com/p/how-to-set-up-and-name-discord-channels) suggests pinning a fill-in template for introductions.

- **What works:** It tells the fan what to write about.
- **What doesn't:** The fan still fills it in and adapts the presentation when interests change. A Discord introduction also stays within one server rather than providing a standalone page to share elsewhere.

### Comparables

**AniList**

On AniList, fans track the anime and manga they have seen. A [profile](https://docs.anilist.co/reference/object/user) has a written bio and favorites picked from the site's database, including characters.

- **What works:** Picking favorites is quick, with nothing to write or design for those selections.
- **What doesn't:** Favorites communicate what someone likes, but do not by themselves explain preferred activities or interaction boundaries. The database focuses on anime and manga.

**AO3 tags**

Archive of Our Own (AO3), a large fanfiction archive, has tags for fandoms, characters, and pairings, and volunteers connect different names for the same thing ([wrangling guidelines](https://archiveofourown.org/wrangling_guidelines/2)).

- **What works:** Its tagging approach accommodates specific fandom interests and alternative names.
- **What doesn't:** The tags describe stories rather than helping an author assemble a personal introduction. It is a useful model for organizing interests, rather than a direct bio-creation alternative.

**AI writing assistants**

A chatbot can help write a bio from a prompt.

- **What works:** It can help someone get past the blank page and organize their wording.
- **What doesn't:** The fan still has to explain their interests, check that the output represents them accurately, and put the text into a visual introduction.

### The Gap

| Alternative | Guidance about what to include | Expresses specific fandom interests | Presentation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Platform bio | Usually an open text field | Written manually | Platform-controlled |
| Carrd, Straw.page, Rentry | Templates or a blank page | Written manually | Author adapts the page |
| Copied format | Section headings and examples | Filled in manually | Author adapts the format |
| AniList | Structured favorite selections and a bio | Database-backed favorites | Platform-controlled |
| AI writing assistants | Suggestions based on a prompt | Depends on the input and output | Usually requires a separate design step |

These alternatives already work well for many fans. Our target is fans who want a personalized introduction but find deciding its structure and arranging its contents burdensome.

Fandom Friends would combine selecting specific interests with an automatically arranged, customizable introduction. A fan could choose interests such as "Fern and Stark" or "sketch swaps," add personal text and interaction preferences, and produce a finished card without positioning each element.

## Solution Sketch

Fandom Friends helps users conveniently create, maintain, and share introduction cards through platforms they already use.

### 1. Describing interests

**Tagging** associates selected interests with a saved bio. Users choose fandoms, characters, pairings, and activities such as "drawing" or "going to cons."

**Synonymizing** connects alternative names for the same interest, while **Grouping** organizes related interests to help users browse the available tags. The initial collection and these relationships are maintained by the team; the application does not rely on an AI agent to decide whether two interests are equivalent.

### 2. Saving and customizing bios

**Drafting** lets users keep several named bios with different tag selections. **Templating** arranges a bio into a finished layout and lets users choose a template, font, and color palette with a live preview.

**UserDisplaying** maintains a display name, optional personal note, and social links. The note can communicate interaction preferences, such as whether direct messages are welcome. Personal information and boundaries are optional rather than required disclosures.

In the current design, the display name, personal note, and links are shared across a user's saved bios, while tags and appearance belong to each individual bio.

### 3. Publishing and sharing

**Publishing** lets users choose one saved bio to display at a shareable address. Other saved bios remain private. Users can switch which bio is published or unpublish it.

The published card is a web page with readable text. Users share its link through their existing social accounts. Saving changes to the published bio updates the public card, so the editor clearly identifies when a user is editing the published version.

Friend discovery, recommendations, mutual matching, and in-app messaging are outside the current scope. If included, "Open to New Friends" is an optional statement on the card rather than enrollment in a public listing or search feature.

## Ethical Analysis

1. **Dimension: Values, Prompt: Value Tensions**

   **Observation:** Public bio cards and social links help users express themselves, but may expose fandom interests to audiences they did not intend.

   **Design response:** Users choose what to include and preview their card before publishing. Other saved cards remain unpublished. The editor identifies the public bio and explains that saving changes to it, or changing shared personal information, affects what readers see. Publishing does not automatically indicate that direct messages are welcome.

2. **Dimension: Stakeholders, Prompt: Indirect Stakeholders**

   **Observation:** Linking someone else's social account on a bio card could make a nonuser appear responsible for that profile.

   **Design response:** We would label links as user-provided and unverified, and let people report impersonation without creating an account. Reviewing reports before removing cards would protect affected people while limiting abuse of the reporting system.

3. **Dimension: Stakeholders, Prompt: Variation in Human Ability**

   **Observation:** A visually styled introduction may be difficult to read if it relies on images of text or has poor contrast.

   **Design response:** Published cards would use real text that screen readers and translation tools can access. Templates would provide readable defaults, and customization would be checked for contrast and readability. The current scope does not include PNG export.

4. **Dimension: Time, Prompt: Choosing Not to Use**

   **Observation:** Users may stop wanting to share an introduction, while previously shared links or copies remain in circulation.

   **Design response:** Users can unpublish their hosted card and remove personal details and links. We would explain that screenshots and other copies shared elsewhere may remain. Account deletion remains a development task rather than a capability already provided by this design.

5. **Dimension: Pervasiveness, Prompt: Widespread Use**

   **Observation:** If many people rely on a team-maintained tag collection, omitted niche interests may become harder to express.

   **Design response:** We would provide a way to suggest missing tags and allow users to describe unlisted interests in their personal note. Alternative-name relationships would be curated and correctable rather than automatically inferred by an AI agent.

6. **Dimension: Stakeholders, Prompt: Vulnerable Stakeholders**

   **Observation:** Public introductions can expose personal information, and fandom communities include minors.

   **Design response:** The initial application is intended for adults. We still need to determine how this policy is communicated and enforced; it does not by itself prevent minors from accessing public links. We would avoid requiring exact ages, locations, or other sensitive details and make clear that published cards can be read by anyone with their address.
